** Humor/Comedy

    *Satire: Uses wit and irony to critique or mock societal shortcomings, institutions, or human folly. Examples include works like A Modest Proposal by Jonathan Swift.
    
    *Farce: A form of low comedy that uses highly improbable situations, exaggerated characters, physical humor (slapstick), and chaotic plots for amusement.
    
    *Romantic Comedy: A lighthearted plot centered on the development of a romantic relationship, typically ending with a happy conclusion.
    
    *Black Comedy (Dark Humor): Finds humor in morbid or taboo subjects such as death, crime, or other sensitive topics. The humor arises from the discomfort and absurdity of the situations.
    
    *Comedy of Manners: A form of high comedy that uses sophisticated language, wit, and subtle characterizations to satirize the behaviors and conventions of a particular social class.

    *Observational Humor: Focuses on everyday life and social norms, pointing out the humor in common situations and shared experiences. Stand up comedy like Jerry Seinfeld's act.
    
    *Absurdist/Surreal Humor: Focuses on illogical events and bizarre situations, often defying rational explanation. Monty Python is a prime example.
    
    *Slapstick: Relies on physical actions, pratfalls, and exaggerated violence for laughs (e.g., The Three Stooges).
    
    **Deadpan: A form of delivery where humor is presented without emotion or a change in facial expression, forcing the audience to find the humor in the statement/action itself.

--	Ch 2	---------------------
Summary:
The Director shows how babies are conditioned to like or hate certain things. In the nursery, Deltas crawl toward flowers and books but are scared by alarms and shocked, so they learn to avoid both. The staff explains sleep-teaching, or hypnopaedia, which does not teach facts but can teach morals through repeated phrases. Children hear lessons like “Everyone belongs to everyone else,” and class pride is built with lines praising their own caste and criticizing others. Conditioning replaces family and personal choice with automatic responses.


Major Topics:
- Behavioral conditioning and the manufacture of desire: Neo-Pavlovian techniques and sleep-teaching demonstrate how preferences and aversions are engineered to support consumption and obedience.
- Erasure of nature and thought: Aversion to flowers and books polices both the natural world and independent inquiry, replacing curiosity with reflexive compliance.
- Infantilization as policy: Moral education stabilizes society by arresting moral development, keeping citizens in a perpetual, cheerful immaturity.

	What happens to the Delta babies when they crawl toward flowers and books, and what is the goal of this lesson?


---	Ch 3	--------------------------
Summary:
Lenina and her friend Fanny talk about dating and the rule that “everyone belongs to everyone else,” while the Controller, Mustapha Mond, lectures students on why the old world of family, religion, and strong emotions was rejected. Children engage in erotic play, showing how early sexuality is normalized. Bernard Marx feels uncomfortable with casual attitudes toward people and sex, especially when he hears Henry Foster talk about Lenina like a product. The chapter contrasts the society’s promise of happiness with the loss of privacy, deep feelings, and history.

viviparous -- bringing forth live young that have developed inside the body of the parent.

Major Topics:
- Historical amnesia and the abolition of the family: Official discourse casts the past and kinship as obscene impediments to “stability,” severing memory and intimate bonds to prevent dissent.
- Sex as social glue: Promiscuity is normalized as a civic duty, transforming eros into a regulatory mechanism that dissipates private loyalties.
- Religion transmuted into consumer-cult: The substitution of technological and economic values for transcendence reframes meaning as efficiency and pleasure.

	Why is “history is bunk” an important idea for the World State?
	What does the phrase “everyone belongs to everyone else” mean in practice?
	How does the society use pleasure to keep people stable?


---	Ch 4	----------------------------------
Summary:
Lenina makes plans with Henry and casually accepts Bernard’s offer to visit the Savage Reservation, embarrassing him by speaking openly in front of others. Bernard feels small and out of place, preferring to be alone and to resist social norms. Later, Bernard talks with Helmholtz Watson, an Alpha Plus writer who also feels something is missing from his life. Though more successful and confident than Bernard, Helmholtz senses he has the power to write something meaningful but lacks the right words. Both men feel alien in a world that demands constant conformity.

	prophylactic
	
Major Topics:
- Alienation within engineered happiness: Bernard’s discomfort exposes the cost of enforced uniformity and the pressure to perform contentment.
- Soma and flight from authenticity: The chemical shortcut to contentment functions as a prophylactic against self-knowledge and sustained reflection.
- Merit and embodiment: Physical engineering and beauty standards conflate moral worth with design specifications, marginalizing deviance as defect.

	What does the chapter suggest about loneliness in a world of constant activity?


---	Ch 5	--------------------------
Summary:
Lenina and Henry fly over London, passing a crematorium that recovers phosphorus from the dead and discussing games like Obstacle Golf that keep people busy. Their date ends in an expected sexual encounter, showing how routine intimacy is. Bernard attends a Solidarity Service meant to create unity through soma, hymns to Ford, and a final “orgy-porgy.” Despite all the noise, drugs, and ritual, Bernard feels more isolated than ever, disturbed by Morgana’s odd eyebrow and his own lack of true connection. The fake unity fails to comfort him.

Major Topics:
- Industrial ecology of death: The crematorium’s phosphorus recovery literalizes the conversion of human life into resource streams, effacing mourning with utility.
- Ritualized pseudo-spirituality: The Solidarity Service commodifies transcendence into synchronized intoxication, simulating community while neutralizing individuality.
- Aesthetic anesthesia: Synthetic entertainments domesticate the sublime, replacing difficult feeling with orchestrated euphoria.

	What is the purpose of the Solidarity Service Bernard attends?
	What role does soma play in the service?


---	Ch 6	------------------------------------------------
Summary:
Lenina and Bernard go out again, but they disagree about pleasure and silence; she wants soma and fun, while he wants to talk and be alone by the sea. Bernard meets with the Director to get permission for the Savage Reservation, and the Director angrily warns him about nonconformity, telling a story about a girl he once lost in New Mexico. He threatens to send Bernard to Iceland for his behavior. Despite this, Bernard and Lenina leave for the Reservation, and Bernard worries about his future as they fly to New Mexico.

Major Topics:
- Conformity enforced by exile: Administrative threats expose the soft authoritarianism underpinning “happiness,” where deviance is exported rather than debated.
- Residual humanity among rulers: The DHC’s confession of past vulnerability reveals the repressed emotional substrate beneath technocratic certainty.
- Colonial gaze and managed otherness: Travel to the Reservation frames nonconforming lifeways as spectacle, confirming metropolitan superiority.

	How do Lenina and Bernard’s ideas about a good date differ?
	How does Lenina react to Bernard’s desire for silence and seriousness?
