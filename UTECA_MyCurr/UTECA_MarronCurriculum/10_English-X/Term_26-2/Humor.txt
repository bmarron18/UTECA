** Humor/Comedy
    *Satire: Uses wit and irony to critique or mock societal shortcomings, institutions, or human folly. Examples include works like A Modest Proposal by Jonathan Swift.
    
    *Farce: A form of low comedy that uses highly improbable situations, exaggerated characters, physical humor (slapstick), and chaotic plots for amusement.
    
    *Romantic Comedy: A lighthearted plot centered on the development of a romantic relationship, typically ending with a happy conclusion.
    
    *Black Comedy (Dark Humor): Finds humor in morbid or taboo subjects such as death, crime, or other sensitive topics. The humor arises from the discomfort and absurdity of the situations.
    
    *Comedy of Manners: A form of high comedy that uses sophisticated language, wit, and subtle characterizations to satirize the behaviors and conventions of a particular social class.

    *Observational Humor: Focuses on everyday life and social norms, pointing out the humor in common situations and shared experiences. Stand up comedy like Jerry Seinfeld's act.
    
    *Absurdist/Surreal Humor: Focuses on illogical events and bizarre situations, often defying rational explanation. Monty Python is a prime example.
    
    *Slapstick: Relies on physical actions, pratfalls, and exaggerated violence for laughs (e.g., The Three Stooges).
    
    **Deadpan: A form of delivery where humor is presented without emotion or a change in facial expression, forcing the audience to find the humor in the statement/action itself.

** "Brave New World" by Aldous Huxley
	Summaries and Topics
		Student responses

###########
Chapter 7
##########
Summary:
At the Savage Reservation of Malpais, Lenina is shocked by aging, disease, and mothers with babies. She and Bernard watch a ritual where a man is whipped, which makes Lenina ill. They meet John, a young man who speaks English, and Linda, a woman from the World State who was left behind years ago. Linda explains that she had a baby there—John—fathered by the Director, and that life in Malpais was harsh for her. The chapter reveals the clash between the World State’s rules and traditional human life.

Major Topics:
- Mortality and the “obscene” body: Aging, childbirth, and disease return as scandal to a culture that has pathologized the natural lifecycle.
- Taboo and longing: Linda’s maternal bond and shame dramatize the persistence of human attachments the World State seeks to abolish.
- Liminal identity: John’s position between worlds exposes the violence of purity regimes that require absolute inclusion or exclusion.


##########
Chapter 8
##########
Summary:
John tells Bernard about growing up as an outsider in Malpais, bullied by other boys and ashamed of Linda’s behavior. He learns to read from old books and becomes inspired by Shakespeare, which gives him a powerful language for his feelings. He tries to join the men’s ceremonies but is rejected. He remembers attacking Popé after finding him with Linda. John dreams of visiting the “brave new world” his mother described, hoping to find belonging and nobility there. Bernard sees a chance to change his own fate by bringing John back.

Major Topics:
- Bildung against the grain: John’s self-fashioning through stories, labor, and renunciation contrasts with hypnopaedia, suggesting education as encounter rather than programming.
- Language as liberation and wound: Shakespeare furnishes a lexicon for desire, honor, and suffering that empowers John while isolating him from both cultures.
- Masculinity, restraint, and violence: Competing codes—initiation rites, jealousy, asceticism—stage the struggle between self-mastery and social acceptance.


###########
Chapter 9
###########
Summary:
While Lenina sleeps off a heavy dose of soma, John breaks into her room, overwhelmed by love and wonder. He admires her silently and restrains himself, repeating “O brave new world” as he imagines a future in London. Bernard works to get permission from Mustapha Mond to bring John and Linda back as a scientific curiosity. With approval secured, Bernard plans to expose the Director’s secret. John and Linda agree to go, setting up a dramatic return to the World State.

Major Topics:
- Instrumentalization of persons: Bernard’s plan to repatriate Linda and John as leverage converts lives into career capital, mirroring systemic utilitarianism.
- Desire and idealization: John’s chaste adoration of Lenina opposes the society’s commodified sexuality, revealing eros as ethical tension rather than mere gratification.
- Thresholds and intrusion: Crossing private and cultural boundaries dramatizes the novel’s preoccupation with spectatorship and violation.


###########
Chapter 10
###########
Summary:
At the London Hatchery, the Director plans to fire Bernard in front of everyone and send him to Iceland. Bernard spoils the plan by bringing Linda and John. Linda exposes the Director as John’s father, shocking everyone because parenthood is shameful in this society. The Director is publicly disgraced, and Bernard’s status rises. Linda, broken by years on the Reservation, chooses a permanent soma holiday.

Major Topics:
- Fragility of authority: The Controller’s system tolerates scandal until a core taboo—parenthood—is exposed, showing that legitimacy depends on maintaining collective disgust.
- Spectacle justice: Public humiliation replaces due process, making governance a theater of shame that disciplines through collective voyeurism.
- Power’s hypocrisy: The DHC’s fall critiques leaders who police transgressions they privately commit, undermining the moral foundation of the regime.


###########
Chapter 11
###########
Summary:
Bernard becomes fashionable because everyone wants to see “the Savage,” but John dislikes being shown off. Bernard brags about his new popularity, which annoys Helmholtz. John visits places like Eton and a feelie and finds the culture shallow and controlled. Mustapha Mond quietly censors science and art to keep order. Linda is kept on soma in the Hospital for the Dying, slipping away from reality.

Major Topics:
- Celebrity and the commodification of difference: John becomes a consumable curiosity, turning critique into a fashion that reinforces the market.
- Addiction as policy: Linda’s perpetual soma-holiday exemplifies institutionalized escapism that substitutes anesthesia for care.
- Art’s unintelligibility under technocracy: The culture’s inability to receive Shakespeare signals the incompatibility of complex art with engineered affect.


###########
Chapter 12
############
Summary:
Bernard throws a party and promises to present John, but John refuses to come out, humiliating Bernard in front of important guests, including the Arch-Community-Songster. Bernard’s popularity collapses. Meanwhile, Helmholtz and John bond over poetry, especially Shakespeare. Helmholtz laughs at parts he finds silly, but he respects John’s passion for language, while Bernard grows jealous.

Major Topics:
- Ephemerality of status: Bernard’s rise and fall expose fame as a function of novelty and supply-demand rather than virtue or insight.
- Fellowship through outlawed meaning: Helmholtz and John’s bond forms around proscribed language, suggesting that genuine community requires risk and shared depth.
- Envy and smallness under mass culture: Petty rivalries thrive where higher aims are unavailable or punished, shrinking the moral horizon.
